My father ate fast. Always. Not because he was in a hurry — but because that was how his father ate, and his father before him. At the table, no one talked about food: food either was or it wasn't. When it was, you ate. The rules existed but were never spoken. They were simply enacted.

I ate the same way. Fast, silent, without questions to the plate. For many years I thought it was just a habit. Then I understood it was an inheritance — precise and heavy, like silver spoons no one polishes but no one throws away either.

## What gets passed across the table.

What gets passed across the table is not a recipe. It's an attitude. The way a father looks at his portion. What a mother says or doesn't say about her own body. The silence that falls after certain questions. All of it settles into children — slowly, without words, permanently.

This is not the father's fault. He received it the same way and didn't choose it either. But now that we can see it, we have an option he didn't: to stop and decide what to pass on.

## The plate I'm leaving behind.

Irina is thirty-one. She eats without argument. I didn't teach her that deliberately — I just stopped arguing myself, in front of her, at some point. That's the whole pedagogy I know: not words, but how you sit at the table.

My father's plate is heavy. But you can set it down.
